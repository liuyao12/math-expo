# Linear Algebra

*From Euclidean geometry to higher and even infinite-dimensional spaces.*

A draft linear algebra textbook that begins with Euclidean geometry in three and higher dimensions, then develops inner product spaces using tangent planes, dual measurements, functions, and Fourier approximation.

The website sources are in `course-site/`. Chapter sketches and essential definitions are in `course-site/chapters.js`; the reader and interactive figures are in `course-site/app.js`. All mathematical notation uses LaTeX and is typeset by the vendored KaTeX renderer.

Run `npm run check` to validate the chapter mathematics, then `npm run build` to produce the static website in `dist/`. To preview, serve that directory with `python3 -m http.server 8000 --directory dist --bind 127.0.0.1`.

The projection animation and its static companion are in `assets/`. Regenerate them with `python3 scripts/render_projection.py` in a Python environment with Pillow. The static image is used when the animation is paused or reduced motion is preferred.

This independent textbook project is part of the shared [Math Expo repository](https://github.com/liuyao12/math-expo). Its root GitHub Actions workflow publishes the text at [Math Expo / Textbooks](https://liuyao12.github.io/math-expo/textbooks/). General vector spaces, groups, and finite geometries are planned later chapters.

The hat–turtle example uses a fixed fourteen-vertex lift from the existing geometric-tree-search project. `course-site/hat-turtle.js` contains its coordinates and projection. The original lift is due to Nan Ma and Arnaud Chéritat; the retained hatviz license is in `course-site/vendor/hatviz/LICENSE`. Checks verify the lift has rank four and that its projected edge lengths agree with the viewing matrix.

The opening uses the convex regular polytope classification to motivate geometry in four dimensions. The `higher-geometry.js` figures compare regular polytope shadows and ball/cube volumes. Lattices introduce the hat–turtle construction after the Gram matrix.

Each reader page has a persistent row/column-matrix toggle. The vector \(v\), its coordinate list \(x=v.c\), and its one-row matrix \([x]\) are distinct. Literal lists always stay fixed; the toggle transposes only matrix representations and reverses the factor order of compositions. Both modes use ordinary multiplication and row-first indexing. Inline matrices remain nested lists even when they have a single row. Gram and projection equations use named matrices \(X,Y,C,D\); scalar pairings identify \(1\times1\) matrices with their sole entries. Checks cover dimensions, mixed Gram pairings, basis changes, projection residuals, scalar extraction, and the live tile matrix. Stable per-entry tags animate rectangular and square transposes.

The lattice chapter’s `tile-lattice-demo.js` animates the viewing angle, displays the live projection matrix, and plots a finite sample of the rank-four lattice. Dot opacity uses the two perpendicular coordinates; a movable round window makes depth visible, with the starting radius enclosing every tile vertex. The full projected lattice is discrete when the tangent of the viewing angle divided by the square root of three is rational, and dense otherwise. A separate interior rational preset makes clear that hat and turtle are not the only discrete views. This is a lattice-window illustration, not a generated hat tiling.


Editorial convention: abstract functions evaluate on the right, \(v.L.K\), with \(v.(LK)=(v.L).K\). Coordinate maps are named functions rather than basis subscripts. The left superscript \({}^{g}\!f=g\,f\,\rho(g)^{-1}\) is reserved for a later induced group action; group-action material remains deferred.


The inner product chapter develops the general real theory in its main text: axioms, norms and angles, orthogonality, Gram matrices, and finite-dimensional projection. Each section of the inner product chapter offers three collapsible right-margin examples: the plane, the sphere’s tangent space, and interval functions. Opening any example closes its companions. The sphere opens first for duality, while functions open first for orthogonality and projection. The geometric example is the plane \(x+y+z=0\), with oblique coordinates \([s,t]\), metric \(G=[[2,1],[1,2]]\), linear functions, and constant one-forms. The duality section defines lowering by fixing one input of the inner product, then computes its column as \(G[x]^{\mathsf T}\); raising reverses this with \(G^{-1}\). Literal row/column descriptions in that section remain invariant under the representation toggle. A quadratic polynomial in a disclosure separates values, differentials, and metric-dependent gradients. Continuous functions on \([0,\pi]\) are a second example for orthogonality and projection, including the finite-dimensional sine/cosine plane. The looping GIF accompanies orthogonality; it needs no slider and has a static reduced-motion companion. The Fourier chapter then uses the full-period interval \([0,2\pi]\), with its corresponding normalization. Regenerate the GIF with Pillow using the function-inner-product renderer in the scripts directory; mathematical labels remain LaTeX in the reader.


The transition to abstraction now begins with concrete row vectors and matrix operations. The coordinate chapter first computes the plane lift from two components to three, reconstructs the same displacement in a second tangent frame, and only then introduces abstract vectors and coordinate maps. Its rectangular lift and composition formulas follow the row/column toggle. The inner product chapter offers exclusive plane, sphere, and function examples. The sphere at \(p=[1,1,1]/\sqrt3\) recovers the plane \(x+y+z=0\); its side examples distinguish the affine tangent plane from tangent vectors, coordinate functions from differentials, and the coordinate-independent metric from its changing Gram matrix. Projection of the ambient coordinate axis constructs \((\mathrm dx_p)^\sharp\). The sphere diagram is a code-generated SVG with all mathematical labels typeset separately in LaTeX.


The sphere example in the covector and projection sections now loops automatically: a point follows a smooth closed path through the diagonal point and both first-coordinate poles, carrying its tangent plane. All three coordinate one-forms are available as level sets, with live scalar measurements of a purple tangent vector. Their projected metric representatives are blue, green, and orange arrows. Selecting a coordinate changes its level sets and dashed ambient direction. The point-dependent identity \(x\,\mathrm dx+y\,\mathrm dy+z\,\mathrm dz=0\) appears as both canceling scalar measurements and a closing head-to-tail triangle of the weighted gradient vectors. Numerical contributions are rounded and marked as approximate; the symbolic relation is exact. No arrow denotes the one-form itself. Motion pauses offscreen and in collapsed panels, stops for reduced-motion preferences, and has a pause/play control. The mathematical model and level-set construction are exported by `sphere-tangent-demo.js` for independent checks.


The sphere view supports mouse, pen, and touch drag to orbit the camera, with arrow-key rotation and Home to restore the initial view. Dragging holds the point temporarily, then resumes its previous motion state. The nearby Face tangent plane control pauses the point and looks along its radius; Reset view changes only the camera. The separate weighted-vector triangle retains its intrinsic plane view. Camera-frame checks include polar views and verify that looking face-on preserves tangent lengths and angles.
